Read the full case

In one line: Donors don't trust that their money arrives, and in crypto a normal wait looks like a failure. Huma is a wallet where people hold a US-dollar stablecoin, send it, and donate to community centres, with a receipt for where the money went. I lead its design on phone and desktop, own its design system, and set the rules the team builds by.

Thirty-second version

  • Problem. Donors don't trust that their money arrives, and crypto makes it worse: blockchain waits look like failures. The client wanted one wallet for giving, sending and earning points, for people with no crypto knowledge.
  • What I did. Designed the consumer app across 43 pages and built the design system behind it. Made one transaction tracker for every money flow, so a wait reads as progress, not a problem. Audited every screen against the product spec instead of guessing, and kept open questions visible instead of inventing answers. I was hired for the interface; I brought the UX in one piece of evidence at a time, until the work needed a second designer.
  • Where it stands. The baseline audit found 45 spec items met, 37 partly met and 16 missing. After my fixes, all 99 automated checks passed. Those are build checks, not user results. Before launch I set pass bars, modelled a crypto donation at about 15 seconds of hands-on time, and wrote a five-person test to replace the model.

A wait that looks like a failure is the whole problem.

A donor picks a community centre, enters fifty dollars and taps donate. If they pay with crypto, the next minute is a blockchain confirming the transfer. Most wallets show a hash and a spinner. To someone who has never used crypto, that looks like their money vanished.

The model depends on people trusting a product built on a technology they don't understand. So the design question was not "how do we make crypto look friendly". It was "how does someone always know where their money is, what happens next, and whether they need to do anything?"

One tracker replaced four waiting screens.

Donate, send, swap and buy all wait on a network. Early prototypes had a different waiting screen for each, and one was a modal that trapped people. One tracker replaced them:

  • A plain headline and a time estimate instead of a transaction hash: "Your donation is on its way. Usually takes 2–5 minutes."
  • Named stages, each saying whether the person needs to act. Most say "Nothing more is needed from you."
  • Designed states for running, delayed, failed and done. A delay gets its own message, so slow never reads as broken.
  • A pending pill in the navigation and a notification, so people can leave the page.

People commit after they've seen the cost, so the gates moved to the end.

The first flows asked people to connect a wallet and pass identity checks before they could see anything. We moved those gates to the last responsible step: browse, pick an amount, see the full cost, then commit.

An unexpected wallet pop-up feels like a scam, so every review screen says how many confirmations to expect, or that there are none.

Phase 1 takes cards only. The other methods stay visible and say Coming soon, so the roadmap is honest and nobody taps into a dead end.

I checked the work against the spec, not against my memory.

The spec owner's feedback kept turning up gaps, so I stopped reviewing screens by eye and audited the whole donation flow against the requirements, line by line.

The last line matters most. Where the spec was silent, I marked the gap and asked, instead of filling it with a confident guess the engineers would then build.

Mobile and desktop are one design with two sets of rules.

The app is responsive web, designed mobile-first and checked on the product owner's device list: 360, 375, 390, 393, 402 and 414 px wide, plus tablets. The 23 batches of mobile feedback were mostly about density and fit, so the repeat issues became a written mobile rule set. A one-screen fit pass at 1280 × 720 took desktop overflow from 380–1,500 px down to mostly zero. An automated check of the Points pages passed at all 154 page and screen-size combinations. Two gaps are still open: contrast has not been re-measured, and screen-reader testing has not been done.

Planned, not built. I also drafted the plan for a native app: navigation model, MVP scope, where iOS and Android conventions should differ from the web, and accessibility requirements. The client has not started building it.

I was hired to make screens. Evidence made the case for UX.

The client brought me in as a UI-focused designer: the brief was the interface. Nobody had asked for research, audits or a states standard, and I didn't ask for permission to start them. I added one practice at a time, and each one earned the next because it caught something the team would otherwise have built.

I own the flows and the rules. The junior designer owns the bad days inside them.

Forty-three pages, each with its own ways to go wrong, is more than one person can design well alongside the flows. So when the junior designer joined, I split the work into two lanes. I keep the flows, the patterns and the design system. The junior designer takes the error and edge states, inside rules I wrote first.

The rules both lanes work to are in the design manual, so a review checks the rule and not my taste:

  • Every failure case the spec names gets its own state, marked as blocking or not. A blocking state disables the main button.
  • Failure, waiting and success are different components. A failed donation never borrows the success badge.
  • Every sentence says what is happening to the money and whether the person needs to do anything.

Decision log

Decision Options What I chose and why
Waiting screens One per flow / one shared tracker Shared tracker. One mental model for users, one component for engineering.
Where to gate wallet and identity checks At the door / near the final step Near the final step. A teammate proposed it in the 20 June huddle and the team agreed. People commit after they've seen the cost, not before.
Unsupported payment methods Hide them / show as Coming soon Show them. Honest about the roadmap, no dead ends.
Donation steps 4 steps / 5 steps 5 steps. I merged them to 4, then went back to 5 so Donate matches Buy. Consistency beat a shorter count.
Balance hero on the home screen Keep / remove Removed after one day. The feedback that prompted it was about the issuer portal, not the wallet. I caught it in the next review.
Points feature copy Promise future perks / describe only what exists Only what exists. I cut multiplier and discount claims the spec didn't support.
Who designs the failure states Me, after the flows / a second designer, in parallel, to written rules In parallel. The states were designed alongside the flows instead of after them, and the written rules kept both lanes in one voice.

The design system separates brand colour from money colour.

Huma runs on a design system I built and maintain: 11 components, about 140 tokens across 4 token files, and 22 guideline cards, with written rules for when a pattern becomes a component. Its most useful rule is small: brand colour is for identity, money colour is for amounts and states, and money colour never decorates. It stops a balance looking like a promotion.

How I led

  • Wrote the standard down. Colour rules, flow anatomy, the tracker pattern, writing rules and a pre-change checklist live in one design manual. My AI tools and I both work from it, so a review is about the rule, not my taste.
  • Turned feedback into rules. Repeat comments from 23 batches of mobile feedback became a written rule set, so the same comment doesn't come back twice.
  • Shared the work with a second designer. As the product grew, a part-time junior designer joined to cover error and edge states. I split the work by lane, set the states standard they work to, and review their work against it.
  • Kept the copy honest. AI helps me draft fast, so I added a writing check to the build that flags inflated or robotic phrasing before anything ships.

Before launch: a bar, a model, and the test that replaces the model.

Nothing is live, so there are no results to show. What I can do before launch is set the bar, model the main task, and write the test that will replace the model with real data.

Measure Bar Receipt
Hands-on time to give 50 USDC from a centre's page, up to the wallet signature About 14.6 s: 5 clicks, 2 keystrokes Modelled
First-time crypto users who finish a card donation unaided 4 of 5 Target
Testers who can say, during the wait, whether they need to do anything 4 of 5 Target
Testers who leave the page mid-wait and find their donation again 4 of 5 Target
System Usability Scale across the session 70 or higher Target

Read these carefully. The model is a keystroke-level model with standard times: 1.1 s to point, 0.2 s to click, 0.28 s per keystroke, 0.4 s to move a hand between mouse and keyboard, and 1.35 s to decide before each step. It leaves out reading, network waits and the wallet app, so real times will be longer. It is there to compare designs, not to predict a result. The targets are mine and still need the product owner's agreement.

The five-person test script
Who Five people who gave to a charity online in the past year. At least three have never used a crypto wallet.
Setup Moderated, 40 minutes, prototype with a test wallet. Think aloud.
Task 1 Give $50 by card to a centre you'd like to support. Measure: finished unaided, time on task.
Task 2 Give 20 USDC. While it confirms, tell me what is happening. Measure: can they say whether they need to act?
Task 3 Leave the page, come back and check it arrived. Measure: do they find it through the pending pill or the bell?
Task 4 Find your receipt and tell me where the money went. Measure: answer checked against the receipt.
After SUS questionnaire, then "What would make you give again?"
If a bar is missed Change the design, then retest with five new people. The model is replaced by these results.

What I'd do differently

I reversed some decisions within a day or two: the balance hero, the number of donate steps (five, then four, then five again), a separate portfolio page. Each reversal was right, but together they cost the engineers time. Most came from feedback meant for a different product, or from me acting on a comment before checking which screen it was about. I now confirm the scope of every piece of feedback in the review itself, before I change anything.

The other gap is research. Everything here was checked against the spec and the team's judgement, not against real users. Moderated sessions with first-time crypto users are next, and the tracker is the first thing I'd test.

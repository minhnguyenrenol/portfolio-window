I raise the bar by making it easier to meet.

Lead designers get judged on the work a team does when they are not in the room. So most of what I do as a lead is written down: the standard, the decisions, the states a screen must have before it ships. Then I coach people against it on real work.

Five rules I lead by

Agree the bar before the screens
Product, engineering and I write down what good looks like and how we will measure it. On RegShield that was a Bronze ExQual target agreed with operations before the redesign; the rebuild scored 4.6, Platinum. It turns “I don’t like it” into “it doesn’t meet what we agreed”.
Write every decision down
Options, the choice, the reason, who asked. RegShield has 23 of these from 27 sessions. People argue once, and nobody re-opens a settled call by accident.
Review against rules, not taste
At Unera the rule is that no module goes to engineering without its error, edge, offline and expired-session states. That rule is why a junior designer can own those states and get useful reviews from me. We talk about the user’s situation, not my preferences.
Make objections cheap
The most useful comment on the stablecoin portal came from a back-end engineer. Engineers raise problems early when they have seen the last one change the product, so I make sure they see it.
Say where AI was used
I tell the team which parts AI drafted and ask them to cut what nobody needs. At one Unera review they removed four features before anyone built them.

How I give feedback

On the exposure check at NAB, a designer I worked with had bold ideas, and some of them moved away from how bankers work. Bankers said the tool no longer felt built around them. I didn’t overrule her in a review. I brought her into the banker feedback sessions so she could hear it first-hand, with ground rules for those sessions, and I briefed her on every update. From then on we designed her screens together, starting from what bankers told us.

When I teach a skill, I use three steps. On merchant onboarding I mentored Ami on UX audits, user testing and synthesis. She watched me run one. We ran the next one together. Then she ran her own, and we reviewed it.

At Unera I review the junior designer’s states against the written states rule, so the feedback is about the user’s situation and not my taste.

Bringing AI into a design chapter

Since March 2026 I have led AI adoption for NAB Vietnam’s design chapter. I didn’t start with a talk about tools. I designed the chapter’s Cursor session around our own work, then coached people one-to-one on whatever they were designing that week.

I also co-ran four workshops with NAB Tech Academy, one of them on wireframing with Cursor for business analysts and product owners. The point was to let them show an idea instead of describing it.

The rule I teach is the one I follow on RegShield: AI drafts, summarises and builds prototypes, but every decision traces to a person. In a regulated product, “the model suggested it” is not a reason anyone can audit.
